<p>Effects moved from glass paintings, miniatures and optical compositing to digital matte painting, CG and real-time engines. The tools changed; the core did not: shoot a believable plate, then make the composite obey the same light, shadow, haze and lens.</p>

<section><h2 id="section-1">What changed</h2><h2 id="section-2">How to use it now</h2><ul><li>Record lens, height, aperture, white balance and on-set light for the plate; the composite needs evidence.</li><li>For CG integration, match black level, light direction and haze before detailing materials.</li><li>When studying effects history, read craft, cost and audience expectation together, not a list of “first CG shots.”</li></ul><h2 id="section-3">Do not misread</h2><p>Do not bury a wrong plate under more layers. If the plate is wrong, a finer composite only looks faker.</p></section><section><h2 id="section-4">On set</h2><p>From glass paintings and miniatures to optical compositing and CG. Watch the optical printer seams in Star Wars.</p></section><section><h2 id="section-5">Do this</h2><p>VFX history runs from glass paintings and miniatures to CG; tools changed, the problem stayed: making the fake believable.</p><ol><li>Build a timeline across glass painting, miniatures, optical compositing, CG.</li><li>Inspect Star Wars optical printer traces, such as matte lines.</li><li>Log one problem each era could not solve and the next one did.</li></ol></section><section><h2 id="section-6">Avoid</h2><p>Do not write VFX history as software versions; the problem stayed, only tools changed.</p></section><section><p><strong>Use it today</strong></p><p>Put the history back into a job: archive, restoration, teaching or a short film. Ask what it solved, then what it still cannot do.</p><ul><li>Find one contemporary work and mark one old limit it inherited.</li><li>Find one condition it no longer needs and say why.</li><li>Write one thing it still cannot do today.</li></ul></section><section><h2>Source boundary</h2><p>This is interpretation, not a primary archive.
