<p>Muybridge used multiple cameras in sequence to break a galloping horse into comparable instants. For the first time motion could be inspected frame by frame, feeding later cinema, animation and motion study.</p>

<section><h2 id="section-1">What changed</h2><h2 id="section-2">How to use it now</h2><ul><li>For animation or motion reference, shoot or find frame-by-frame reference; do not invent a cycle by feel.</li><li>When breaking down an action, mark contact, lift-off and weight transfer before choosing frame counts.</li><li>When studying early images, remember it was scientific observation, not narrative cinema.</li></ul><h2 id="section-3">Do not misread</h2><p>Do not treat frame-by-frame reference as a style filter. Its value is believable motion, not an aged look.</p></section><section><h2 id="section-4">On set</h2><p>The Horse in Motion used 24 cameras along the track, each triggered once. Watch the grid and each frame position; it was not one continuous camera.</p></section><section><h2 id="section-5">Do this</h2><p>Muybridge used a row of cameras to split a horse's motion, showing what the eye was too slow to see.</p><ol><li>Place 24 cameras along the track, each firing once.</li><li>Put a grid behind the subject to compare positions frame by frame.</li><li>View the 24 frames in order, not as one continuous camera.</li></ol></section><section><h2 id="section-6">Avoid</h2><p>Do not treat The Horse in Motion as one continuous camera; it is 24 cameras each exposing once.</p></section><section><p><strong>Use it today</strong></p><p>Put the history back into a job: archive, restoration, teaching or a short film.
