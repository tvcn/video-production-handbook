<p>The Lumière train film brought the audience into a real space: fixed camera, full depth, movement from far to near. It proved cinema could record something happening, not only a stage performance.</p>

<section><h2 id="section-1">What changed</h2><h2 id="section-2">How to use it now</h2><ul><li>For a real location, find the camera position where action happens naturally before designing complex movement.</li><li>For a documentary opening, establish space with one complete action, then cut to detail.</li><li>When studying early cinema, note how the fixed frame and deep staging create tension, not only the “audience panicked” legend.</li></ul><h2 id="section-3">Do not misread</h2><p>Do not treat a fixed camera as laziness. It demands blocking, light and action that work inside one frame.</p><h2 id="section-4">The Lumières' value is not “the first screening” but taking the camera into the street</h2>
<p>The 1895 screening is often called the birth of cinema. More important: they moved the machine out of the studio and filmed trains, factories and streets. Real scene, single shot, fixed camera: the first repeatable production model.</p>
<h3>Common misreading</h3>
<p>“The Lumières only shot documentary” is inaccurate; they also made staged comic shorts. Another myth: “audiences ran from the arriving train.” It was exaggerated later and should not be cited as fact.</p>
<h3>One use today</h3>
<p>With one camera, find a real, repeatable action, place the camera and wait. Do not start with complex blocking.</p></section><section><h2 id="section-5">On set</h2><p>Arrival of a Train used a fixed camera and diagonal depth. The audience feared the train coming at the lens, not 3D.</p></section><section><h2 id="section-6">Do this</h2><p>The Lumières projected a train arriving, and audiences were frightened by something fake for the first time.</p><ol><li>Lock the camera and let the train approach on a diagonal into depth.</li><li>Do not cut; hold one long take.</li><li>Note that the fear comes from motion toward the lens, not 3D.</li></ol></section><section><h2 id="section-7">Avoid</h2><p>Do not attribute the reaction to 3D; it is a train coming toward the camera.</p></section><section><h2>Timeline</h2><p>Three nodes only; each needs one “replaced” and one “added” line.</p><ul><li>1895: return to the text and name one old problem it replaced and one new limit it added.</li></ul></section><section><p><strong>Use it today</strong></p><p>Put the history back into a job: archive, restoration, teaching or a short film.
