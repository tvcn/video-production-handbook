<h2>On set</h2>
<p>Start each scene with interior or exterior, location, and time. Keep action blocks under four lines and capitalize a character name on first appearance. The sound team needs to know when the door opens.</p>
<h2>Do this</h2>
<p>Script format is not for a literary prize; it helps camera, sound, and edit find information at a glance.</p><ol><li>Open every scene with interior or exterior, location, and time.</li><li>Keep action blocks under four lines; capitalize a character name on first appearance.</li><li>Put door, phone, and footstep cues into the action so sound knows what to capture.</li></ol>
<h2>Avoid</h2>
<p>Do not write a novel; if the crew cannot find scene numbers and sound cues, the set will miss them.</p>
<h2>Check</h2>
<ul><li>Every scene has interior or exterior, location, and time with continuous numbering.</li><li>Action blocks stay under four lines and first character names are capitalized.</li><li>Sound can mark at least three cues to record from the script.</li></ul>

## Deep dive
<p><strong>About 6 minutes.</strong> The field note tells you how to write. This layer is about one page that lets five people start work at once, and which words to cut when you rewrite.</p>
### One page, five people using it at once
<p>The director looks for action, the camera department for space, sound for lines, art for props, and the editor for scenes. A script is not literature; it is a work order five people can read at once.</p><p>A scene heading carries four facts: interior or exterior, location, time. Action describes only what the camera can see, and a character name is capitalized on first appearance. Keep each dialogue line under roughly thirty-five Chinese characters; long lines make actors gasp and subtitles overflow.</p><blockquote>INT. KITCHEN — NIGHT<br>The kettle whistles. She kills the flame, picks up her phone, and deletes the message she just wrote.</blockquote>
### Rewrite only three kinds of words
<p>Cut explanatory adjectives, repeated names, and inner states the actor can play. Change “she angrily slams the door” to “she slams the door; it bounces back once.” Precise action gives the actor something to do.</p><p>Solve one class of problem per pass: one pass removes explanation, the next fixes pace, the next rewrites lines. Change three things at once and tomorrow you will not know who wrote which line.</p>
### Do not drop scene headings; the edit finds footage by them
<p>Scene numbers are not for the director; they let script supervisors, editors, and post find footage. Start a new scene whenever time or place changes. Never replace a heading with “later.”</p><p>Separate day and night inside the same location. An editor looking for “kitchen night” does not want to read a page of prose.</p>
### Page count and shoot days
<p>A page is roughly a minute on screen, but takes one to three hours on set depending on setups, cast, and weather. Three pages of dialogue are not automatically one day.</p><p>Schedule by pages times difficulty, not by scene count. Ten empty shots can take half a day; one crowd scene can take two.</p>
### Ten checks before you print
<ul><li>Scene heading names interior or exterior, place, and time</li><li>Action describes only what the camera can see</li><li>Capitalize a character name on first appearance</li><li>Keep each dialogue line under thirty-five characters</li><li>Give each scene one turn, not two</li><li>Delete explanatory adjectives</li><li>Turn inner states into playable action</li><li>Keep scene numbers continuous from first to last</li><li>Mark pickups and flashbacks separately</li><li>Leave a notes column for the script supervisor</li></ul><p>If two of these ten fail, do not start a new scene. Make this page shootable; that is worth more than three more pages.</p>
